## Defence on a Shoestring: What the Mid-Term Budget Means for the SANDF

Parliament’s defence committee asked for answers on 16 October. It wanted the finance and defence ministers to agree on costs and dates for ending the department’s salary overspend from 2027/28. It also raised reports that deployed soldiers lack boots, uniforms, armour, weapons and working vehicles. Five days later, Finance Minister Enoch Godongwana tabled his mid-term budget. For defence, it was a disappointing reply.

### A bigger number, but not much more money

This year’s Defence vote rises from R57.6 billion to R61.5 billion, a 6.7% jump. Look closer and most of it evaporates. About R2 billion is income the department earns itself, chiefly UN and SADC reimbursements for the DRC deployment, plus equipment and spares sales. Only R823 million is new, funding troops who assist police against illegal mining and gangs in five provinces. That is less than 2% of the extra R45.5 billion that the Treasury is spending this year.

Even so, the vote sits below the R62.3 billion spent last year. Salaries are the pressure point. The department budgeted R37.8 billion but spent R40.4 billion last year. By September, 55% of the annual allocation had been spent, indicating an overspend of around R3.8 billion. This is the gap the committee wants closed, and the budget doesn’t close it.

The longer view is no better. The wider defence and state security function climbs from R62.4 billion to R65.5 billion by 2029/30, dipping to R61.3 billion in 2027/28. That is about 1.6% a year. With inflation forecast at 3.8%, 3.2% and 3.1% over the next three years, the final-year budget buys roughly 5% less than today’s.

### Why Treasury holds the line

Treasury’s logic isn’t hard to follow. Interest costs on debt total R441.5 billion this year, about R1.2 billion a day, while defence receives roughly R168 million a day. A R54.1 billion revenue windfall did not shrink the R301.6 billion deficit, because it was absorbed by other spending. Defence wasn’t at the front of the queue.

Others fared better. SAA received a R1 billion guarantee in August for working capital after the fuel price shock. Treasury’s director-general, Duncan Pieterse, said it wasn’t a cash payment, would not rule out more support, and noted the airline has absorbed about R50 billion over the years. The MTBPS also lists a R10 billion guarantee for the Land Bank, R2.2 billion for Ithala, further backing for Sanral, and R889 million in guarantees for Denel’s secured contracts.

A guarantee costs nothing until it is called, whereas the soldiers’ money is cash. But Treasury itself cautions that falling guarantee usage flatters the picture, partly because the state has already paid out. SAA shows that calls do come. Together, the new guarantees for SAA, the Land Bank and Ithala come to R13.2 billion, about 16 times what was found for the soldiers. Even Denel’s guarantee alone exceeds it.

### The cost of doing less

The consequences are visible. In 2025/26, the air force planned 12,000 flying hours and managed 6,340, or 52.8%, which its annual report attributes to late contracts and insufficient funds for maintenance and parts. The Airbus Helicopters and Denel Aerospace agreement on helicopter serviceability is welcome, but a partnership can speed repairs without paying for the parts.

There is also a risk of R2 billion in own income. It depends on UN and SADC budgets and on a foreign deployment Pretoria doesn’t control. If that income shrinks, so does operating money, while troops are also deployed at home.

### What Parliament should demand

The 2015 Defence Review defined the capabilities the country needs, but they have never been funded, and this budget doesn’t fund them either. A force cannot be sized for its mandate and paid for a smaller one. Hattingh argues Parliament should ask both ministers for a published list of what R61.5 billion buys and what it doesn’t, and insist that the salary overspend be budgeted for in February rather than rediscovered in the adjustments.

Public finances are steadier than three years ago, but part of that stability has been bought by asking the defence force to do more with less, and by guarantees that stay off the books until someone calls them. Godongwana may be entitled to hold that line, but the real cost will only show when the country needs the SANDF and finds it cannot deploy.
